P64: Abstract
P65: Conventional machine learning paradigms require centralizing sensitive patient data on a single server, a model that fundamentally conflicts with modern privacy, sovereignty, and security mandates in healthcare. While superficial solutions such as column-level deletion (anonymization) or transport encryption appear expedient, empirical studies demonstrate that de-identified datasets remain vulnerable to correlation and re-identification attacks (Sweeney, 2000). Moreover, encryption-at-rest or in-transit still necessitates decryption at the central server for model training, creating a single point of compromise and violating data-locality requirements under HIPAA, GDPR, and Pakistan's emerging National Health Data Policy.
P66: Federated Learning (FL) resolves this algorithmically by training models directly on end-user devices without raw data transmission; yet existing frameworks, Flower, OpenFL, FedScale, provide only research-grade algorithms, leaving a critical infrastructure vacuum: no production-ready orchestration, automated failure recovery, declarative configuration, or MLOps pipeline integration exists.
P67: FL-KubeOps addresses this gap by building a Kubernetes-native operator (kopf framework) and Kubeflow-integrated MLOps pipeline specifically designed for privacy-preserving federated learning across distributed healthcare nodes. The system architecture comprises three co-designed layers: The four simulated hospital clients will train on their local partition of approved chest X-ray image(s). Raw images as well as the patient's identifiers shall stay in the assigned client node. The synthetic data shall be sent only to automated tests and not supplied as hospital data rev and not proven against the hospital-data benchmark. The EfficientNet-B0 classifier shall predict one finding from the proof of concept, selected after reviewing approved X-ray labels and image quality. A separate chat interface operating locally will answer questions about FL-KubeOps and explain the presented classifier result. The chat interface shall not diagnose disease, interpret an image beyond the classifier, recommend triage or treatment, or aid in clinical decision making.
P68: FL-KubeOps is a research prototype for running federated-learning rounds across four simulated hospital clients on a K3s cluster. The proof of concept uses chest X-ray images from Shaukat Khanum Memorial Cancer Hospital and Research Centre (SKMCH&RC), subject to written data-use approval. The model will classify one finding selected after review of the approved labels and image quality. The system includes a locally run chat interface that explains the classifier’s output and answers questions about system operation. The prototype is not for diagnosis, triage, treatment recommendations, or clinical decision-making.
P69: The design targets automatic recovery from node failures within 60 seconds, tracking of every training round in MLflow, and deployment on commodity hardware from a single declarative YAML manifest, reducing FL deployment from weeks of manual scripting to minutes of infrastructure-as-code.

P72: Product Description:
P73: Healthcare institutions generate the richest machine-learning training data available, patient records, laboratory results, diagnostic imaging metadata, and clinical notes. Yet regulations such as the U.S. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), and Pakistan's emerging National Health Data Policy mandate strict data locality: patient information must not leave the facility of origin in any decipherable form. A hospital in Lahore cannot legally transmit patient records to a shared server in Islamabad, even to train a model that would benefit all participating institutions. Consequently, each hospital trains clinical AI models in isolation, producing statistically weak models that fail to generalize across population diversity, a critical deficiency in a country like Pakistan where disease phenotypes vary significantly across provinces.
P74: A common objection, raised during the Idea Defence, is that this problem could be solved through simpler means: deleting columns containing personally identifiable information (PII) or applying column-level encryption before transmission. While these approaches present superficial appeal, empirical studies demonstrate their inadequacy for healthcare AI. First, removing sensitive columns (diagnoses, lab values, demographic markers) strips the very features that carry the highest predictive signal for clinical models; a diabetes-prediction model cannot function without glucose readings. Second, correlation attacks and re-identification remain potent threats: Sweeney (2000) famously demonstrated that 87% of the U.S. population could be uniquely identified using only zip code, gender, and date of birth. In Pakistan's close-knit demographic contexts, quasi-identifiers such as age, gender, caste, and admission date are highly distinctive. Later re-identification studies of hospital discharge data have shown that even "de-identified" datasets can be linked to auxiliary public records. Third, encryption-at-rest or in-transit still requires decryption at the central server for gradient computation, creating a single point of compromise and violating the principle of data sovereignty. Fourth, differential privacy noise injection, while mathematically rigorous, degrades model utility in low-sample clinical settings; homomorphic encryption, while secure, is orders of magnitude slower than federated learning and infeasible for deep neural networks on commodity hardware.

P100: The regulatory requirement is not merely that data be "hard to read" during transit, but that data sovereignty be preserved, data must never leave the facility of origin in a form that permits reconstruction of individual records. Federated Learning (McMahan et al., 2017) satisfies this requirement by construction: only model gradients (mathematical weight updates) traverse the network, never raw patient records. However, the algorithm alone is not deployable. No production-ready infrastructure exists to orchestrate real FL deployments: Kubernetes has no native concept of a federated training round; Kubeflow Pipelines assumes centralized data access; and existing FL frameworks (Flower, OpenFL) provide client-server communication libraries but treat Kubernetes as a passive deployment environment rather than an orchestration primitive. This structural gap, an algorithm without an operating system, forces hospital IT teams to manually manage client lifecycles, handle mid-round node failures, coordinate aggregation timing, and track model versions: engineering overhead that no clinical institution can sustainably absorb.

P147: Literature Review:
P149: Federated Learning was introduced by McMahan et al. (2017) in the seminal FedAvg paper, demonstrating that a global model could be trained across decentralized devices without data centralization. Bonawitz et al. (2017) extended this with practical Secure Aggregation, using pairwise masks and threshold secret sharing to ensure that the server learns only the aggregated sum of client updates, not individual contributions. Since then, the field has expanded into differential privacy (Abadi et al., 2016), system-level frameworks (Li et al., 2020), and domain-specific healthcare applications (Rieke et al., 2020).
P150: On the infrastructure side, Flower (Beutel et al., 2020) and OpenFL (Reina et al., 2021) have emerged as the dominant open-source FL frameworks. Flower provides a flexible Python client-server API with pluggable aggregation strategies; OpenFL offers a peer-to-peer topology with Intel-optimized secure aggregation. Flower provides a threshold-based secure aggregation protocol (SecAgg+, based on Bonawitz et al. 2017) with dropout tolerance, but both frameworks remain research-oriented. An analysis of open GitHub issues (as of July 2026) reveals recurring requests for Kubernetes operator integration, automated node recovery, and pipeline orchestration, features that remain unaddressed in their core roadmaps. Kubeflow (Google, 2024) provides mature MLOps pipeline primitives (TFJob, PyTorchJob) but assumes centralized data sources and offers no first-class support for federated round scheduling or cross-node gradient aggregation. Kubernetes Operators (Dobies & Wood, 2021) have proven effective for complex stateful workloads (databases, message queues), yet no operator exists that makes FL a Kubernetes-native primitive, orchestrating rounds with the same declarative rigor that TFJob brings to distributed TensorFlow.
P151: The gap is infrastructural, not algorithmic: federated learning libraries exist, but no production orchestration layer automates deployment, recovery, versioning, and compliance audit at hospital scale. FL-KubeOps fills this by integrating Flower's SecAgg+ protocol into a Kubernetes operator that treats federated rounds as first-class CRD resources, with Kubeflow pipeline tracking and MLflow experiment logging.

P213: Proposed Solution:
P215: FL-KubeOps is a Kubernetes-native operator and Kubeflow-integrated MLOps pipeline designed specifically for federated learning orchestration among dispersed healthcare nodes. A hospital IT team defines an FL Cluster custom resource, a single declarative manifest that specifies participating client nodes, aggregation strategy (FedAvg, FedProx), model architecture, and security parameters. The system converges to the desired state and maintains it autonomously over the entire FL lifecycle.
P216: The architecture comprises three integrated layers that are co-designed and mutually dependent:
P217: Client Layer: Simulated hospital nodes run as Docker containers orchestrated by K3s (lightweight Kubernetes). Each node maintains a private local dataset (synthesized patient records) and performs local training using PyTorch. Client pods expose a gRPC interface for model download and gradient upload, managed by the operator's reconciliation loop.
P218: Server/Orchestration Layer: A kopf-based Kubernetes operator watches FL Cluster resources and executes the federated lifecycle: broadcasting the global model, collecting encrypted client updates, triggering aggregation, incrementing round counters, and persisting results to MLflow. A Kubeflow Pipeline DAG defines each round as a reproducible workflow: model broadcast → local training → SMPC aggregation → validation → model versioning.
P219: Security Layer: Flower SecAgg+ is used for secure aggregation. Client nodes send protocol messages and masked model-update contributions. The aggregation service computes the secure aggregate and releases a global model update only when at least three of the four clients complete the configured protocol. It does not receive raw X-ray images, patient keys, clinical histories, or individual unmasked updates. This security claim applies to the configured protocol and its stated threat assumptions. The system does not implement differential privacy.
P220: Clinical AI Validation: Chest X-ray Classification: GenAI suggests machine learning algorithms present in health care (a chest x-ray classifier identifying pneumonia and 13 other pathologies from an x-ray), The title’s GenAI component refers to a locally run, bounded chat interface; the X-ray classifier is a predictive model, not a generative model. For this, we use a CNN with approximately 5.3 million parameters, pre-trained on the Shaukat Khanum Memorial Cancer Hospital and Research Centre dataset, based on EfficientNet-B0. It serves three functions. First, it shows that FL-KubeOps is at the production scale, not linear regressors on CSV file types but multi-layer CNNs on image data. Second, federated learning is a classic application of medical imaging, where there are real regulatory bottlenecks: This prototype evaluates data-locality controls; it does not establish regulatory compliance or authorize clinical use., but a model trained on one hospital's X-rays fails to generalize to a hospital's patient population. Third, the benchmark is reproducible because the X-Ray dataset is public and peer-reviewed.
P221: No layer in a distributed system can be removed without compromising its integrity; removing the client layer undermines data privacy, while eliminating the server layer disrupts model convergence and pipeline reliability. Federated Learning's value lies in the protocol binding clients and servers, As with TCP/IP or HTTP, coordination between components is essential.

P328: Objective of the System
P329: The system is designed to achieve five quantifiable outcomes:
P330: Kubernetes-Native Operator: Develop a kopf-based FL Cluster Custom Resource Definition (CRD) with a reconciliation loop that manages the complete federated lifecycle (create, update, delete) on a live K3s cluster without manual intervention.
P331: Reproducible MLOps Pipeline: Construct a Kubeflow Pipeline DAG that executes federated training rounds (model broadcast → local training → SMPC aggregation → validation) with all metrics and model versions tracked in MLflow.
P332: Cryptographically Secure Aggregation: Integrate PySyft SMPC so that individual client model updates cannot be recovered from the aggregation server unless a threshold of shares is combined, checked by inspection of intermediate data at the aggregation server.
P333: Automate Fault Recovery: Implement automatic detection and recovery from simulated client pod failures mid-round, with recovery time not exceeding 60 seconds from failure onset.
P334: Clinical Benchmark Validation: The federated model’s AUROC on the same patient-disjoint test set shall be no more than 0.05 below the centralized model’s AUROC.
P335: User Research
P336: Primary Research: Structured Interview with Industry Practitioner
P337: A structured, semi-directive interview was conducted on 15 July 2026 with a Senior Machine Learning Engineer at Arbisoft, Lahore, software product company with active healthcare AI client engagements in North America and the Middle East. The interview followed the four-question protocol: (1) What operational barriers prevent FL adoption in production environments? (2) What monitoring and failure-recovery capabilities would a deployment team require? (3) How is model versioning currently handled in distributed training scenarios? (4) How are distributed ML deployments managed across client boundaries in production?
P338: Key Findings:
P339: Declarative Deployment Abstraction: The interviewee emphasized that current FL frameworks require "weeks of Python scripting just to handle pod restarts and model checkpointing." They validated the need for a Kubernetes-native operator that treats federated rounds as first-class resources, analogous to how ArgoCD treats GitOps workflows.
P340: Observability Gap: Standard monitoring tools (Prometheus, Grafana) lack awareness of federated round state. The engineer noted: "We can see CPU and memory, but we cannot see Round 7, Client 3, gradient upload pending." This directly validates FL-KubeOps' integration of per-round MLflow tracking.
P341: Model Versioning Complexity: In distributed training, model version drift across nodes is a critical issue. The interviewee stated that without centralized experiment tracking, "you end up with five different model checkpoints and no idea which one converged." MLflow integration addresses this explicitly.
P342: On-Call Burden: Manual failure handling in distributed training requires 24/7 on-call engineering time that hospital IT departments cannot allocate. Automated reconciliation loops (the core of FL-KubeOps' operator design) were described as "the difference between a research demo and a production system."
P343: Expected Benefits:
P344: Operational Efficiency: Reduces FL deployment time from weeks of manual scripting per hospital to minutes via a single “kubectl apply -f FL Cluster.yaml” command. Beneficiaries: Hospital IT Departments, MLOps Engineers.
P345: Regulatory Compliance: Enables compliance with HIPAA, GDPR, and Pakistan's National Health Data Policy by ensuring no raw patient data traverses network boundaries. Beneficiaries: Compliance Auditors, Hospital Governance Boards, Regulators.
P346: Clinical Model Utility: Achieves ≥85% of centralized model accuracy by pooling population-scale diversity without data centralization, producing models that generalize across demographic segments. Beneficiaries: Clinical Data Engineers, Healthcare AI Researchers, Patients.
P347: Infrastructure Democratization: Packages production-grade FL expertise into declarative infrastructure, lowering the barrier to entry for institutions without specialized distributed-systems teams. Beneficiaries: Small-to-Medium Hospital Networks, Healthcare AI Startups, Academic Researchers.
P348: Fault Tolerance: Automatic recovery from node failures (including load-shedding-induced reboots common in Pakistan) ensures training continuity without 24/7 on-call engineering. Beneficiaries: System Administrators, Hospital IT Staff.

P416: Non-functional Requirements
P417: Reliability
P418: NFR-R01: The kopf-based operator reconciliation loop shall execute continuously with a watch stream timeout recovery mechanism, ensuring no missed cluster state transitions.
P419: NFR-R02: If a client pod fails while its K3s agent node and local data remain available, the operator shall restore the client within 60 seconds of failure detection. The system shall not move the client’s dataset to another node.
P420: NFR-R03: A federated round shall complete only when at least three of the four clients complete the configured secure-aggregation protocol before the round deadline. If fewer than three clients complete, the round shall fail, no new global model shall be published, and the last committed model shall remain active. A recovered client may join a later round after its health and local dataset are checked.
P421: NFR-R04: The system shall accept at most one valid update from each client in a round. It shall reject or ignore duplicate updates and reject updates for a closed round, a different model version, or an invalid round.
P422: Performance
P423: NFR-P01: The system shall report per-round and total duration for a ten-round, four-client X-ray benchmark. The report shall state the image count per client, image size, batch size, local epochs, GPU model, and software versions.
P424: NFR-P02: Kubeflow Pipeline DAG execution overhead (scheduling, artifact passing, step initialization) shall not exceed 10% of total round time.
P425: NFR-P03: The MLflow dashboard shall load experiment lists within 3 seconds for up to 100 logged rounds.
P426: NFR-P04: gRPC control-plane communication latency between operator and client pods shall remain below 100 ms on a local K3s cluster.
P427: Use-ability
P428: NFR-U01: A complete FL cluster shall be deployable via a single declarative YAML manifest  without requiring manual pod creation or script execution.
P429: NFR-U02: The Kubeflow Dashboard shall be accessible via standard browser (Chrome, Firefox) without additional plugins, showing pipeline run status, step logs, and artifact links.
P430: NFR-U03: The MLflow UI shall present per-round metrics (accuracy, loss, communication overhead) in sortable, filterable tables with downloadable CSV export.
P431: NFR-U04: All error messages from the operator and pipeline components shall include actionable remediation steps (e.g., "Client pod failed liveness probe: check ConfigMap dataset-path").
P432: Other Non-functional Requirements
P433: Security Requirements
P434: NFR-S01 (Data Sovereignty): Raw X-ray images, patient keys, and clinical histories shall remain on their assigned client nodes. Only secure-aggregation protocol messages, masked model-update contributions, and the coordination data required to run a round may be sent to the aggregation service.
P435: NFR-S02 (SecAgg+ Irretrievability): Secure-aggregation verification shall check the configured Flower protocol, the messages exchanged, and the behavior with four, three, and fewer than three clients. Process-memory inspection shall not be used as proof that individual updates cannot be recovered.
P436: NFR-S03 (Transport Security): All control-plane communication (gRPC, Kubernetes API, MLflow tracking) shall use TLS 1.3 with certificates rotated every 90 days.
P437: NFR-S04 (Audit Logging): Every model broadcast, gradient upload, aggregation event, and RBAC access decision shall be logged with UTC timestamp, actor ID, and action outcome to an immutable append-only log.
P438: NFR-S05 (Secret Management): SMPC seed values and JWT signing keys shall be stored in Kubernetes Secrets, never in ConfigMaps or container images.
P439: Scalability Requirements
P440: NFR-SC01: The minimum viable release shall support four simulated client nodes. The system shall not claim support for an arbitrary number of clients unless that configuration has been tested.
P441: NFR-SC02: The aggregation server shall support pluggable aggregation strategies (FedAvg, FedProx, custom) via a strategy interface, allowing future extension without pipeline recompilation.
P442: Explain ability & Validation Requirements
P443: NFR-E01: Every training round shall produce reproducible metrics logged to MLflow, including random seed, dataset hash, model architecture version, and hyperparameter snapshot.
P444: NFR-E02: Kubeflow Pipeline DAG definitions shall be version-controlled and executable without code changes on any K3s cluster matching the documented version constraints.
P445: NFR-E03: A team member other than the author of the deployment guide shall deploy the system on a clean cluster matching the documented versions. The evaluator shall use the published configuration and test dataset. Equivalent results shall be defined as an absolute AUROC difference of no more than 0.02 from the median of three reference runs.
P446: Compliance Requirements
P447: NFR-C01 (HIPAA/GDPR Data Locality): The minimum viable release shall support four simulated client nodes. The system shall not claim support for an arbitrary number of clients unless that configuration has been tested.
P448: NFR-C02 (Audit Trails for Regulatory Inspection): Compliance auditors shall be able to export a complete, tamper-evident audit trail of all federated rounds (participant nodes, timestamps, SMPC verification hashes) in PDF format suitable for regulatory submission.

P472: Unit Testing
P474: Framework: We use pytest for every test. GitHub Actions is configured to run pytest and flake8 on each PR. A failing test blocks the merge.
P475: Isolation: Mocks and stubs replace external dependencies. Examples: Kubernetes API, gRPC channels, MLflow server. No test needs a running K3s cluster. unittest.mock.
P476: Out of scope: Some behaviour needs a running system. Sections 5.3 and 5.4 verify it. Examples: 60-second recovery, SMPC memory inspection, TLS.
P477: Test sequence: Setup, execute, verify, teardown. Setup builds fixtures, mocks, and a seed. Teardown resets mocks and deletes temporary files.
P478: Process: We write the plan entry before coding. The story's focal person writes the tests. Tests ship in the same pull request. Unit names are placeholders until implementation.

P539: Conventions:
P540: Branching: main is the only long-lived branch. Each user story gets one branch created from main, named feature/US-<ID>-<short-description>. For Bug fixes, use fix/US-<ID>-<short-description>.

Gradient Aggregation | The process of combining model updates (gradients) from multiple clients into a single global update.
Data Sovereignty | The principle that data is subject to the laws and governance structures of the nation or facility where it is physically located.
Differential Privacy | A mathematical framework for sharing information about a dataset by describing patterns of groups while withholding information about individuals.
Homomorphic Encryption | A form of encryption permitting computation on ciphertexts, generating encrypted results that decrypt to match plaintext computation results.

DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) | A graph structure used in pipeline frameworks where nodes represent tasks and edges represent dependencies, with no cyclic dependencies.
